## Making Data and Narrative Building

[Making Data and Narrative Building.ipynb](<Making Data and Narrative Building.ipynb>) reorganizes the analysis in `Map_IpAddresses.ipynb` into tutorial-style stages: import tools, locate files, inspect inputs, transform and validate address intervals, compare geographic relationships, map, and export. Every code block begins with an explanatory comment. The original notebook remains available as the source version.

The central question is how estimated IP locations relate to the administrative geography of networks and selected corporate organizations. Three different forms of evidence are kept separate: a geolocation database estimates where address ranges are located; a BGP snapshot observes which autonomous systems originate routes; provider lists describe the address ranges that particular services publish. Together they support a critical account of network geography. They do not measure legal control, internet traffic, individual users, or server locations.

## Running the analysis

Use the `cdp` Python environment with NumPy, pandas, GeoPandas, Shapely 2, Matplotlib, and IPython installed. Run the notebook from the beginning, in order. Paths are discovered from the assignment folder or a parent directory in the course repository. The analysis reads local snapshots and does not request new data. Large interval tables and the country-dot rendering can take several minutes.

The new notebook exports twenty individual maps, **only as PDF**, directly into `output/maps/`, named `Map01_…` through `Map20_…`. It does not export PNGs or a combined atlas. Derived analytical tables are written to `output/tables/`; `output/metadata/atlas_manifest.json` records the map order and paths relative to `output/`. Old exports from earlier versions can remain in nested folders; they are not the numbered deliverables generated by this notebook.

## From records to comparable quantities

The notebook counts addresses rather than source rows. A row can describe a large or small interval, so counting rows would distort comparisons. IP2Location intervals are sorted and checked for overlap. Known country codes such as `NA` for Namibia are preserved rather than interpreted as missing values.

For routing data, nested prefixes are converted into disjoint intervals using the most specific observed prefix. Single-origin routes can be joined to organization metadata; multi-origin and AS-set records remain ambiguous. Intersecting sorted routing and geolocation intervals counts each overlap once. Unknown organization and ambiguous-origin categories remain visible in the tables.

For the administrative comparison, the denominator is all BGP-observed addresses matched to a known geolocation country, including addresses with an unknown organization or ambiguous origin. The numerator for the foreign share includes only addresses whose known origin-organization country differs from the geolocation country. A low foreign share can therefore coexist with substantial uncertainty. It does not demonstrate domestic ownership.

For provider lists, overlaps are collapsed **within each list**. The four lists remain separate because their scopes differ. Coordinates come from interval intersections with IP2Location, not cloud-region labels. The coverage tables report matched addresses and addresses outside the database's coverage; a successful interval match does not necessarily mean a known country or valid coordinates.

For the company comparison, reference ASNs identify the primary CAIDA organization for Amazon (16509), Meta (32934), Google (15169), and Microsoft (8075). All ASNs sharing each selected organization ID are included. Only single-origin routed intervals join this selection. This creates a consistent selection rule, but omits networks assigned to different subsidiary organization IDs. It is a reproducible organizational sample, not a corporate asset census.

## Visual design

Every map uses a pure black (`#000000`) page and axis background. Country maps use a staggered lattice of circular dots inspired by the Barcelona Dynamics reference. Each dot has a faint halo and a sharp core. The staggered lattice uses 110 km spacing in the projected coordinate system, about 2.5 times as many dots as the previous 175 km lattice. Smaller marks preserve visible gaps. The turquoise, mint, lime, amber, coral, and pink palette is retained; color follows the existing numerical normalization. Dot size and spacing are fixed within each panel and do not encode another variable. The dots are a cartographic texture clipped to country polygons, not observed locations or a local density surface.

Country outlines, polygon fills, and the world graticule are omitted from country maps. A country with zero or missing values has neither pattern nor outline. This removes geographic scaffolding from unrepresented countries, but also makes zero indistinguishable from missing data in the image; consult the derived tables and coverage diagnostics. Only the plotted national values determine which countries appear. The original estimated-location maps retain their actual database-assigned positions and a subdued geographic grid. Map03 uses a single mint color.

## Reading the maps critically

The first three plates retain point geography; subsequent plates aggregate values to countries. The change in scale is an analytical choice: points expose the database's spatial assignments, while polygons support national comparison but conceal local variation.

Absolute counts use logarithmic color scales. Company national shares use a shared power scale (`gamma=0.3`) from 0 to 100 percent, making smaller shares more visible. Color differences are therefore not linearly proportional to percentage-point differences. The foreign-registration map uses a linear percentage scale.

On country maps, circular-dot spacing is constant: **color encodes value; dot density does not**. Large countries accommodate more dots without necessarily holding more addresses. Countries with zero or missing values are both invisible, with no outlines. Their absence should not be read as proof that a company or the internet is absent. The run reports codes without matching polygons, including BQ, GF, GI, GP, MQ, RE, SJ, TK, UM, and YT; AQ is excluded by design. Inspect these diagnostics before treating the rendered world as complete. Robinson projection supports world comparison but distorts shapes and distances; the graticule retained on the point maps is a geographic reference, not a network topology.

## Derived evidence and reproducibility

`output/tables/ipv4_totals_by_country.csv` and `cities.json` document DB11 aggregations. `asn_organizations.csv`, `ipv4_prefixes_organizations.csv.gz`, and `ipv4_routed_intervals.csv.gz` preserve the routing/organization transformations. `ipv4_country_relationships.csv` and `ipv4_country_summary.csv` expose domestic, foreign, unknown, and ambiguous categories behind Map05.

The `bigtech_ipv4_*` tables document published ranges, unions, country counts, and totals. `bigtech_published_locations.csv.gz` and `bigtech_geolocation_coverage.csv` preserve geolocation results and coverage. The `bigtech_selected_asns.csv` and `bigtech_bgp_*` tables record company selection, intervals, locations, national counts, and coverage for Maps11–20. Retaining these tables allows a reader to inspect the evidence beyond the map image.

Earlier outputs such as the GeoPackage, candidate-ASN tables, PNGs, and combined atlas are not all regenerated by the new notebook. Their presence should not be interpreted as proof that they match the current run. The numbered PDFs and current manifest identify this version's map series.

The design suggests relationships between territory, registration, and networks, but the data cannot establish who controls an individual user's connection or where personal data are stored. Those questions require additional evidence about routing paths, traffic, corporate structures, facilities, and legal arrangements. Snapshot mismatches, incomplete organization grouping, and uncertain geolocation remain part of the argument rather than details to hide.
